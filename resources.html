Clinical background

Chronic venous insufficiency: the disease behind the procedure.

A short primer on what CVI is, how it progresses, how clinicians classify it, and where phlebectomy fits among treatment options.

What is chronic venous insufficiency?

Chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) is a form of venous disease that occurs when the veins in the legs are damaged. These veins can no longer manage blood flow as they should, so it becomes harder for blood in the legs to return to the heart. Blood pools in the leg veins, raising the pressure inside them.1

CVI is not only cosmetic. Over time it causes pain, swelling, heaviness in the legs and changes to the skin. Advanced cases can lead to ulceration and significant loss of mobility.

CVI is degenerative. Without treatment it becomes more severe. Early diagnosis and intervention are the key to preventing long term complications and preserving quality of life.

Signs and progression

The most common manifestation of CVI is varicose veins. Other signs include reticular veins and telangiectasias.2

Telangiectasias and reticular veins

Telangiectasias are dilated venules or arterioles under 1 mm. Reticular veins are tortuous subcutaneous veins under 3 mm. Patients may experience itching, burning or pain.3

Varicose veins

Swollen, twisted and bulging blood vessels that usually appear just under the surface of the skin in the legs and feet.2

Venous ulcers

Sores that take weeks or months to heal. They can worsen quickly and put patients at risk of complications, including limb loss.4

Classification

The CEAP score

Clinicians classify chronic venous disease with the CEAP system, a structured way to describe the severity and cause of a patient's disease and to guide treatment. CEAP stands for Clinical, Etiological, Anatomical and Pathophysiological.

E identifies the cause (congenital, primary or secondary). A describes which veins are affected (superficial, deep or perforator). P explains the blood flow disturbance, either reflux or obstruction. The clinical class, C, runs from C0 to C6 and is the part most patients hear about.

Clinical class

C0No visible or palpable signs of venous disease
C1Telangiectasias or reticular veins
C2Varicose veins
C3Edema
C4Skin changes such as pigmentation, eczema or lipodermatosclerosis
C5Healed venous ulcer
C6Active venous ulcer

Treatment options

Where phlebectomy fits

Chronic venous disease is managed with lifestyle changes, compression therapy, medication and a range of minimally invasive procedures. Each option targets symptom relief and long term disease control.

Conservative care

Compression stockings, leg elevation, exercise and weight management to relieve symptoms and slow progression.

Endovenous ablation and sclerotherapy

Thermal or chemical closure of refluxing veins from inside the vessel, commonly used for the main superficial trunks.

H.E.A.L. focus

Phlebectomy

A surgical procedure that removes problematic veins through small incisions. Highly effective for relieving symptoms, restoring appearance and preventing further degeneration. Controlling bleeding during this procedure is the gap the H.E.A.L. clamp system addresses.
